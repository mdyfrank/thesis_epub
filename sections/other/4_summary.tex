\chapter*{Summary}
Recommender systems (RecSys) are widely deployed in online platforms to help users discover relevant items from large collections. Despite their success, they still face the sparsity of observed user-item interactions and the difficulty of incorporating semantic knowledge that interaction records do not express. This dissertation studies how the knowledge held in language models should be coupled with collaborative graph structures, and argues that the appropriate granularity of this coupling depends on the recommendation task. Tighter coupling at the representation level admits richer semantic transfer but raises training cost, while offline relation inference and bounded agentic reranking offer lighter alternatives that remain grounded in the item catalog.

Five frameworks are developed along a progression from structural modeling to semantic guidance and then to explicit reasoning. The first treats pretext tasks and auxiliary relations as hyperedges, so heterogeneous and higher-order signals are unified in one pretraining stage. The second describes each task in plain text, encodes the descriptions with a pretrained language model, and uses the resulting semantics to guide hypergraph pretraining across domains. The third aligns user and item representations with the token embeddings of a large language model (LLM), placing collaborative patterns and pretrained linguistic knowledge in a common space. The fourth queries an LLM offline for substitute and complementary relations between items and uses the inferred relations to weight hyperedges. The fifth moves the language model to decision time, where an agent selects among recommendation tools and reasons over item relations to rerank a bounded candidate set without further training.

Experiments on public benchmarks from Amazon, Steam, Goodreads and Instacart show that each form of coupling improves recommendation quality over the baselines of its own setting, and that no single coupling strategy dominates across tasks. The experiments involve only computational analysis of existing datasets and do not involve human subjects, animals, or recombinant DNA. Therefore, approvals from the Institutional Review Board, the Animal Care Committee, or the Institutional Biosafety Committee are not required.
