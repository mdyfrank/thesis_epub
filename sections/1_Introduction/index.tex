\chapter{Introduction}\label{intro}
\section{Motivation and Dissertation Overview}

\subsection{Motivation and Problem Setting}

In the digital era, recommender systems (RecSys) play a crucial role in helping users discover relevant items across online platforms. By modeling historical user-item interactions, RecSys learn user preferences and provide personalized recommendations. To effectively capture collaborative signals, graph learning has become a dominant paradigm in RecSys, where users and items are organized into a bipartite structure for information propagation~\cite{glrssurvey21}. However, user-item interactions are inherently sparse due to limited user exploration and the extremely large candidate item space. Under such sparse settings, relying solely on pairwise interaction modeling remains insufficient for robust personalization.

Two observations motivate the work in this dissertation. First, the interactions that a platform records are only one view of the relations that connect users and items, since co-purchase behavior, item groupings, item attributes, and auxiliary supervision signals all carry information that a bipartite graph cannot express. Second, much of the knowledge needed to interpret those relations is textual rather than behavioral, because whether two items serve the same purpose, or serve each other, is a property of what the items are and not of how often they happen to appear together. Pretrained language models (PLMs) and large language models (LLMs) hold knowledge of this kind, so the question that organizes this dissertation is how such knowledge can be brought into a recommendation model that must still rank a full catalog efficiently and must still return identifiers that exist in that catalog.

\subsection{Structural Signals, Semantic Knowledge, and Reasoning}

The information brought into recommendation in this dissertation is of three distinct kinds, and keeping the three apart is important for interpreting what each chapter contributes. Structural signals describe which entities co-occur. They are read off observed behavior and side information without any language model, and higher-order structure of this kind is naturally expressed by hypergraphs, where a single hyperedge connects an arbitrary number of nodes. Semantic knowledge describes what entities mean. It is obtained by encoding text with a PLM or an LLM into representations that a recommendation model can consume, and world knowledge is the special case that concerns properties no single dataset states, such as whether one product can replace another. Reasoning differs from both, since it refers to a language model producing an intermediate decision at inference time, for example choosing which retrieval tool suits a particular user or deciding why a candidate should be promoted.

These three kinds of information are used at different points in the following chapters. Chapter~\ref{chpt:uprth} uses structural signals alone. Chapter~\ref{chpt:ihp} adds semantic encoding of task descriptions by a PLM, and Chapter~\ref{chpt:glta} tightens that encoding into a shared representation space with an LLM. Chapter~\ref{chpt:erlsc} draws on world knowledge, but it does so offline and stores the result as structure. Only Chapter~\ref{chpt:agentdr} asks a language model to reason at decision time. Treating semantic representation, world knowledge, and reasoning as one capability would obscure this progression, so the terms are used separately throughout.

\subsection{Thesis Statement and Research Questions}

The central claim of this dissertation is that language model knowledge should be coupled with collaborative graph structures at a granularity that suits the recommendation task, rather than at the tightest granularity that is technically available. Coupling at the representation level, where graph embeddings and language tokens share a space, permits the richest semantic transfer, but it requires gradients to pass through a large model and its cost grows with the model that supplies the knowledge. Coupling through offline relation inference, where a language model is queried once and its output is stored as a fixed structural weight, and coupling through bounded agentic reranking, where a language model reorders a short candidate list returned by conventional retrievers, are lighter and keep the recommended items grounded in the catalog. The five frameworks presented here occupy different points on this spectrum, and the picture that emerges is that the appropriate point depends on the task rather than on the capacity of the language model.

Three research questions organize the work.

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{RQ\arabic*.}, leftmargin=*, itemsep=0.2em, topsep=0.4em]
\item How can higher-order and heterogeneous relations be modeled so that they alleviate the sparsity of user-item interactions rather than introduce noise?
\item At what level should structural representations and language model knowledge be aligned, and what does a tighter alignment cost in training?
\item How can the knowledge and the reasoning ability of LLMs be used in recommendation while preserving grounding in the item catalog and scalable full-catalog ranking?
\end{enumerate}

Chapter~\ref{chpt:uprth} and Chapter~\ref{chpt:ihp} mainly address RQ1, Chapter~\ref{chpt:ihp} and Chapter~\ref{chpt:glta} address RQ2, and Chapter~\ref{chpt:erlsc} and Chapter~\ref{chpt:agentdr} address RQ3. Chapter~\ref{chpt:conclusion} returns to the three questions and reads the five sets of results together.

\subsection{Contributions and Organization}

The main contributions of this dissertation are summarized as follows.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=0.2em, topsep=0.4em]
\item A unified pretraining framework that generalizes heterogeneous pretext tasks as hyperedge prediction and learns the relevance of each task to recommendation through a transitional attention layer.
\item An instruction-based hypergraph pretraining framework that encodes plain-text task descriptions with a PLM and injects them into hyperedge-level propagation for cross-domain recommendation.
\item A token alignment framework that places graph representations and LLM token embeddings in a shared space and predicts items by logits matching instead of free-form generation.
\item A relation-aware hypergraph framework that obtains substitute and complementary judgments from an LLM offline and converts them into adaptive hyperedge weights.
\item An LLM agent framework that delegates full-catalog ranking to conventional recommenders and reasons over substitute and complementary relations to rerank a bounded candidate set, together with an evaluation metric that measures semantic alignment jointly with ranking correctness.
\item A cross-chapter analysis of how the granularity of graph-language coupling trades semantic transfer against computational cost, presented in Chapter~\ref{chpt:conclusion}.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of this dissertation is organized as follows. The rest of this chapter introduces the five works in turn. Chapter~\ref{chpt:uprth} presents UPRTH, Chapter~\ref{chpt:ihp} presents IHP, Chapter~\ref{chpt:glta} presents GLTA, Chapter~\ref{chpt:erlsc} presents ERLSC, and Chapter~\ref{chpt:agentdr} presents AgentDR. Chapter~\ref{chpt:conclusion} synthesizes the findings, discusses limitations, and outlines future directions.

\section{Hypergraph Learning for Relation Modeling in Recommendation}
(Part of this section was previously published in~\cite{uprth}.)

Although graph-based RecSys have shown strong performance, they mainly model pairwise user-item interactions, which limits their ability to capture higher-order relations and heterogeneous signals. In real-world recommendation scenarios, rich relational structures naturally exist, including multi-entity dependencies and auxiliary supervision signals. Hypergraph learning provides a principled framework to model such complex relations, as a hyperedge can connect multiple nodes simultaneously.

In this work, we propose a multitask framework named Unified Pretraining for Recommendation via Task Hypergraphs (UPRTH). To provide a unified learning paradigm for diverse relational signals, we generalize different pretext tasks as hyperedge prediction problems within a task hypergraph. In this way, heterogeneous relations and auxiliary attributes can be integrated into recommendation through a shared hypergraph structure. Furthermore, a transitional attention layer is designed to adaptively learn the relevance between each auxiliary task and the main recommendation objective. Experimental results on three benchmark datasets verify the effectiveness of hypergraph learning for modeling diverse relations in recommendation.

\section{Hypergraph Pretraining Guided by PLM-Encoded Textual Prompts}
(Part of this section was previously published in~\cite{ihp}.)

Building upon the flexibility of hypergraph construction introduced in UPRTH, we extend this paradigm to cross-domain recommendation by incorporating semantic guidance into hypergraph pretraining. In cross-domain settings where domains share the same users but completely different items, discrepancy in data distribution hinders effective knowledge transfer via structural signals alone. To address this limitation, we introduce plain-text prompts that explicitly describe task semantics and domain connections, and encode them using a PLM to guide hypergraph pretraining. To be concrete, we propose Instruction-based Hypergraph Pretraining (IHP), a framework that injects PLM-encoded textual instructions into hypergraph learning to align source-domain knowledge with target-domain recommendation objectives. A prompt hypergraph convolution layer is designed to inject the encoded prompt representations into hyperedge-level information propagation, enabling instruction-aware high-order relation modeling. By integrating semantic guidance from text prompts with structural hypergraph learning, IHP enhances cross-domain knowledge transfer and improves recommendation performance across multiple datasets.

\section{Training Large Recommendation Models via Graph-Language Token Alignment}
(Part of this section was previously published in~\cite{glta}.)

While IHP introduces plain-text prompts to provide task-level semantic guidance for hypergraph learning, the amount of injected knowledge remains limited to a small number of instructions, each compressed into a single fixed-dimensional vector. To integrate richer prior knowledge from language models, we explore a finer alignment between graph-based recommendation models and pretrained language representations. Traditional collaborative filtering models primarily rely on user-item interactions and lack direct access to the extensive semantic knowledge encoded in LLMs. At the same time, directly applying LLMs for recommendation often suffers from ambiguous item generation and poor scalability.
To address these challenges, we propose training large recommendation models via Graph-Language Token Alignment (GLTA). Instead of generating items through free-form text, GLTA aligns user and item representations from the interaction graph with pretrained LLM token embeddings, allowing the graph model to inherit semantic knowledge from frozen LLMs in a structured manner. Furthermore, we introduce Graph-Language Logits Matching (GLLM) to optimize token alignment for end-to-end item prediction, which avoids the ambiguity of text generation while preserving scalability. Experimental results on benchmark datasets demonstrate that token-level alignment effectively enhances recommendation performance.

\section{LLM-Inferred Substitute and Complement Signals for Recommendation}
Token alignment couples the two sides tightly, but it requires backpropagation through the entire language model, and as the size of LLMs continues to grow this becomes increasingly expensive. To obtain a more lightweight integration of LLM knowledge into recommendation, we instead query an LLM offline to evaluate functional relations between items, and enhance recommendation through the refined relational signals that result.

Substitution and complementarity are essential item-item relations for understanding user intent in RecSys. However, reliable annotations of such relations are rarely available in public datasets, and behavior-based signals such as co-view and co-purchase often conflate true semantic relations with incidental co-occurrences. To address this challenge, we propose Enhancing Recommendation with LLM-Inferred Substitute and Complement Signals (ERLSC), a framework that leverages LLMs to assess functional relations within item groups and guide hyperedge weighting. We adopt a hypergraph formulation to represent item groups induced by co-occurrence signals, and use LLMs to evaluate pairwise substitute and complementary relations within each group. Based on these semantic assessments, we design an LLM-guided hypergraph convolution layer that initializes and adaptively updates hyperedge weights, enabling the model to emphasize semantically meaningful item groups while suppressing noisy co-occurrences. Extensive experiments on three public datasets demonstrate that ERLSC consistently outperforms strong baselines, and ablation studies verify the effectiveness of each component.

\section{Dynamic Recommendation with Item-Item Relations via LLM-based Agents}
(Part of this section was previously published in~\cite{agentdr}.)

While ERLSC leverages LLMs to refine structural signals through LLM-guided hyperedge weighting within a unified graph model, it still follows a single-model learning paradigm. In this setting, the final recommendation performance ultimately relies on a single trained model, even when it is enhanced by LLM-derived relational signals. Motivated by the reasoning capabilities of modern LLMs, we instead explore an LLM-based agent framework that can coordinate multiple recommendation tools and perform explicit relational reasoning at inference time, without being restricted to the output of a single recommendation model.

Recent agent-based recommendation methods use memory and prompting to mimic user behavior, but they often hallucinate non-existent items and struggle with full-catalog ranking. To address these challenges, we propose AgentDR, an LLM-agent framework that combines commonsense reasoning with scalable recommendation tools. AgentDR treats the LLM as a high-level decision maker, where full-catalog ranking is delegated to traditional models for efficiency and ID grounding, while the LLM integrates multiple recommendation outputs based on personalized tool suitability. Building on the effectiveness of leveraging LLM world knowledge to evaluate item-item relations in ERLSC, we further enable the agent to explicitly reason over substitute and complementary relations inferred from the user's historical interactions, guiding the selection and refinement of candidate items. This modular design preserves scalability and stability while adding relational reasoning at the decision level. Experiments on three public grocery datasets show that AgentDR improves full-ranking performance, achieving on average a twofold gain over its underlying tools, and we further introduce an LLM-based evaluation metric that jointly measures semantic alignment and ranking correctness.
